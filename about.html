About this calendar

A year that starts when the Earth does

The Gregorian year begins on January 1st, a date that has no meaning in the sky. This calendar begins the year on the spring equinox, divides it into 13 identical months of 28 days, and closes it with one or two Threshold Days. It is a way of keeping time that follows the sun, the seasons and the moon.

The year as a wheel

    ☀︎

    A natural starting line

    At the equinox the sun sits directly over the equator and day and night are close to equal everywhere on Earth. From that moment the days grow longer than the nights. It is a precise astronomical event, not a convention, so the year begins when spring does.

    ▦

    Months that all match

    Each month is exactly four weeks long. The 13th of any month falls on the same weekday as the 13th of every other month that year. There are no 30-or-31 rhymes to remember, and no February. Months are equal, so comparing one with another is fair.

    ◐

    Closer to the moon

    A 28-day month sits between the moon's orbit around the Earth (27.3 days) and its cycle of phases (29.5 days). The moon circles the Earth about 13.4 times a year, so a 13-month year follows its rhythm more closely than a 12-month one.

    ✦

    The year corrects itself

    The Gregorian calendar needs leap-year rules to stay in step with the seasons. This calendar has none. Each year runs from one real equinox to the next, so it is 365 or 366 days by itself. The Threshold Days take up the difference.

    An old idea, not a new one

    Starting the year in spring is how many people kept time for most of history.

    • Nowruz, the Persian New Year, begins at the March equinox. It has been celebrated for over 3,000 years and is still observed by around 300 million people.
    • Babylon opened its year with the Akitu festival in the month of Nisannu, at the spring equinox.
    • The early Roman year began in March. That is why September, October, November and December are named for the numbers 7, 8, 9 and 10. They used to be the seventh to tenth months.
    • England and its colonies began the legal year on March 25th until 1752.
    • The 13 × 28 layout was proposed by Moses Cotsworth in 1902 as the International Fixed Calendar. Eastman Kodak ran its business on it from 1928 to 1989.

    The thirteen months

    The names follow the seasons from spring to spring. Because the year starts at your spring, they fit both hemispheres. In the south the year begins at the September equinox, so Solara still brings the summer sun and Umbra still holds the longest night.

      How it lines up with nature

      Because the year starts on the equinox, the other turning points of the year land in nearly the same place every year. In the Gregorian calendar they also stay in roughly the same place, but on dates no one thinks of as special. Here they mark the year's real structure. Midsummer is always early in Solara, the autumn equinox falls in Messis, the harvest month, and the longest night falls in late Umbra, the month of shadow.

      The moon is shown on every day as it really is, because 28-day months do not lock to the 29.5-day lunar cycle. Each month drifts about a day and a half against the moon, so the full moon moves gradually through the months. That is a real rhythm worth watching too.

      Lunar mode: months that follow the moon

      The 13 × 28 layout keeps every month equal, but it can't keep months in step with the moon. The moon takes about 29.5 days to go from one new moon to the next. Switch to Lunar months and the calendar works the way the Babylonian, Hebrew and Chinese calendars do:

      • The year begins at the first new moon on or after the spring equinox.
      • Every month begins on the day of a new moon and lasts 29 or 30 days.
      • An equinox year holds about 12.4 moon cycles. Most years have 12 months (about 354 days). About one year in three has a 13th month, Renova, when another new moon arrives before the equinox (about 384 days).
      • New Year moves from year to year, but always falls within about 30 days after the equinox (between March 20 and April 19 in the north).

      The trade-off is the reverse of the fixed calendar. The moon and the months always match, but months differ in length and start on different weekdays.

      Honest trade-offs

      • The world runs on Gregorian dates. Use this calendar alongside them, not instead of them. That's why every day shows its Gregorian date.
      • The weekday shifts each year. Within a year every month starts on the same weekday. The Threshold Days are real weekdays here, so the next year starts one or two weekdays later. (Cotsworth's plan kept them outside the week, which breaks the seven-day cycle.)
      • New Year's Day depends on where you are. The equinox is a single instant, so its local date can differ by a day between time zones.
      • Months are not lunar months. 28 days comes close to the moon's cycle but does not match it exactly.
